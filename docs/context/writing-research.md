# Writing guidance research

*Research report, 2026-10-26, compiled by Claude Opus 5.5 from four research threads (style guides, document structure, plain-language standards, and developer and AI writing). It is the evidence for [0029](../decisions/0029-writing-structure-writing-style-two-skills-project-guide-first.md). Kept as written, except that links to unofficial copies of in-copyright books were replaced with citations of the published editions.*

## Summary: build two writing skills from converged guidance

An AI coding agent's writing guidance should be **two short skills, not one**: a structure skill that starts from what the reader needs to do and puts the point first, and a style skill that makes each sentence readable on the first pass. Both should open with the same precedence rule, taken almost word for word from Google's developer style guide: follow the project's own style guidance first, then the skill, and depart from either when that makes the text clearer. Across a century of style guides, military and government standards, documentation frameworks and developer conventions, the sources agree on a small core. On structure: name the reader and their goal, lead with the bottom line, make every heading and list say what it is for, group and order on purpose, and put background last. On sentences: make the actor the subject and the action a verb, put familiar information before new, cut words that do no work, name the specific thing, and use the plain word. The licence picture is workable: **Strunk's 1920 text, US government works (the Federal Plain Language Guidelines and Army Regulation 25-50), OpenAI's CC0 Model Spec, and the CC BY 4.0 Google style guide and GitHub Docs can be quoted**; Diátaxis and Wikipedia (CC BY-SA) and every in-copyright book (Strunk & White, Williams, Zinsser, Minto, Covey, ISO 24495-1) must be paraphrased. Leave out the blanket passive-voice ban, usage folklore, banned-word lists and fixed sentence-length caps, because credible critics or the evidence on AI prose undercut each one. On punctuation, no source bans the em dash, but Strunk, Strunk & White, Williams and Google all choose the mark that joins two clauses by how the clauses relate, and Strunk & White says to use a dash "only when a more common mark of punctuation seems inadequate." That supports the owner's view, and the evidence also shows how to state the rule: say which mark fits which relation, because Wikipedia editors report that bare em-dash bans have flattened AI punctuation to commas and periods.

## Begin with the reader's end: Covey, Minto and BLUF agree on the first move

Stephen Covey's second habit, "Begin with the end in mind", is a general principle about any piece of work, and a document is one. FranklinCovey's own page says it means having **"a clear vision of your desired destination"** and that "having an end in mind for a meeting, project, or a lifetime gives you more clarity about where to start and how to adapt"; its one-line summary is "Define clear measures for success and create a plan to achieve them" ([FranklinCovey, Habit 2](https://www.franklincovey.com/courses/the-7-habits/habit-2/)). For writing, the end is what the reader can do once they have read the thing: approve the PR, run the tool, file a usable bug. Covey supplies the principle; the writing-specific methods supply the mechanics, and each one is that principle applied to text. Strunk & White's composition chapter states it in writing terms: "The first principle of composition, therefore, is to foresee or determine the shape of what is to come and pursue that shape" (Strunk & White, 4th ed., Rule 12 (Strunk and White, *The Elements of Style*, 4th ed., Allyn & Bacon, 2000)).

Barbara Minto's Pyramid Principle gives the method for finding that end: her situation, complication and question framework exists "for identifying the question in your reader's mind", and the document's single governing point is the answer, placed at the top with every supporting idea grouped beneath it ([barbaraminto.com](https://www.barbaraminto.com/)). The US Army makes the same move mandatory. **Army Regulation 25-50, paragraph 1-38b, calls "putting the main point at the beginning of the correspondence (bottom line up front)" one of two essential requirements** of Army writing, and paragraph 1-39b adds "Use enclosures for additional information" ([AR 25-50 (2020)](https://www.maine.gov/dvem/policies/documents/AR%2025-50%20(10%20October%202020).pdf)). Journalism's inverted pyramid puts the five Ws in the lead and orders the rest by decreasing importance, so a reader who stops early still has what matters ([Purdue OWL](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/subject_specific_writing/journalism_and_journalistic_writing/the_inverted_pyramid.html)).

Plain-language standards define success the same way Covey does, as an outcome for the reader. ISO 24495-1:2023 adopts the definition that wording, structure and design are "so clear that intended readers can easily find what they need, understand what they find, and use that information" ([plainlanguage.com](https://plainlanguage.com/what-is-plain-language/iso-plain-language-standard/)), and the Federal Plain Language Guidelines open with "The first rule of plain language is: write for your audience" ([FPLG, Center for Plain Language repost](https://centerforplainlanguage.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/FederalPLGuidelines.pdf)). Diátaxis, the most widely adopted open documentation framework, organizes everything around four user needs and says a how-to guide contains "no digression, explanation, teaching", linking to those instead ([Diátaxis](https://diataxis.fr/how-to-guides/)). DITA's concept topic exists "to introduce the background or overview information for tasks or reference topics" ([OASIS DITA 1.3](https://docs.oasis-open.org/dita/dita/v1.3/os/part2-tech-content/langRef/containers/concept-elements.html)), and Carroll's minimalism tells writers to let users start on meaningful tasks at once ([instructionaldesign.org](https://www.instructionaldesign.org/theories/minimalism/)). **Six independent traditions (self-management, consulting, the military, journalism, plain language and technical documentation) converge on one sequence**: name the reader's goal, state the point or action first, keep doing, looking up and understanding apart, and put background and provenance last.

Developer conventions add one structural rule the others leave implicit: the text must stand on its own. The git project asks that a commit message "can be understood without external resources. Instead of giving a URL to a mailing list archive, summarize the relevant points of the discussion" ([git SubmittingPatches](https://git-scm.com/docs/SubmittingPatches)). Google's code-review guide warns that links "may not be visible to future readers" and lists "Phase 1." among its bad change descriptions ([Google eng-practices](https://google.github.io/eng-practices/review/developer/cl-descriptions.html)). That rule covers a common agent failure, the label coined during a working session that means nothing to anyone who was not there.

## Sentence rules that survived a century of criticism

The classic sentence-level rules hold up better than their reputation, provided a skill keeps the qualifications their authors attached. Geoffrey Pullum, the sharpest modern critic of *The Elements of Style*, calls its style advice "mostly harmless" and "Omit needless words" useless rather than wrong, because "The students who know which words are needless don't need the instruction" ([Pullum 2009](https://www.lel.ed.ac.uk/~gpullum/50years.pdf)). Joseph Williams answers that objection by turning each maxim into something a writer can check: make the main characters the subjects, make their important actions verbs, open sentences with familiar information, push new and complex information to the end, get to the main verb quickly, and concision as six specific deletions (Williams & Bizup, *Style*, 11th ed. (Williams and Bizup, *Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace*, 11th ed., Pearson, 2014)). Those checkable forms are what an agent can apply.

The rules that survive appear in three or more sources each. **Cut words that carry nothing** comes from Strunk's Rule 13, Orwell's rule iii, Williams's Lesson Nine and the FPLG's "Omit unnecessary words" ([Strunk](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/37134); [Orwell](https://www.orwellfoundation.com/the-orwell-foundation/orwell/essays-and-other-works/politics-and-the-english-language/)). **Name the specific thing** is Strunk's Rule 12, "Prefer the specific to the general, the definite to the vague, the concrete to the abstract", and Tatham's "Above all, be precise" for bug reports ([Tatham](https://www.chiark.greenend.org.uk/~sgtatham/bugs.html)). **Put familiar information first and new information last** is Strunk's Rule 18 (the end holds "the new element in the sentence"), Williams's principles 4 and 6, and Pullum's own account of when a passive reads well. **Use the plain word** is Orwell's ii and v and the FPLG's "Use short, simple words", with Google adding that necessary jargon should be defined "clearly on first use" ([Google, Jargon](https://developers.google.com/style/jargon)). **State what is, not what isn't** is Strunk's Rule 11, which allows "not" for denial or contrast but never "as a means of evasion". **Keep subject and verb close** and **keep parallel ideas in parallel form** are Strunk's Rules 16 and 15, Williams's principle 5, and the FPLG's "Keep subject, verb, and object close together."

The evidence on AI prose makes two of these rules more important for an agent than for a human writer. Reinhart and colleagues found that **instruction-tuned models use nominalizations at 1.5 to 2 times the human rate and present participial clauses at 2 to 5 times the human rate** ([Reinhart et al., PNAS 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11874169/)). Williams's rule "make important actions verbs" targets the first directly, and Wikipedia's catalogue of AI tells names the second: superficial analysis tacked onto sentences as "-ing" phrases such as "highlighting" or "ensuring" ([Wikipedia: Signs of AI writing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing)). The same catalogue reports that models avoid plain "is" and "has" in favour of "serves as" or "boasts", and that human writers use plain verbs ("used" rather than "utilized"). The plain-word rule addresses that habit.

The same study also cuts against the most famous rule. **GPT-4o uses agentless passives at roughly half the human rate** ([Reinhart et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11874169/)), so a passive ban would target a problem AI text does not have. Pullum calls the charges against the passive "entirely baseless" and counts more passives in Orwell's own essay than in typical prose ([Pullum, passive paper](https://pullum.ppls.ed.ac.uk/passive_loathing.html)). Strunk himself said the passive is "frequently convenient and sometimes necessary." What survives is the version Google states: "Use active voice: make clear who's performing the action" ([Google, Highlights](https://developers.google.com/style/highlights)). Williams supplies the test: does the reader need to know who acted, and which voice keeps the familiar term at the front of the sentence?

## Punctuation should name the relation between a point and its elaboration

The owner's objection to the em dash is that it does one job for every relation, so the reader cannot tell whether the second half explains, qualifies, illustrates, contrasts with or follows from the first. The sources do not ban the dash, but they share the principle underneath the objection: **the mark that joins two statements should be chosen by how they relate**. Strunk's Rule 5 recommends the semicolon for two closely related clauses and explains why: it "suggests the close relationship between the two statements", and "this simple method of indicating relationship between statements is one of the most useful devices of composition. The relationship, as above, is commonly one of cause or of consequence" ([Strunk 1920](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/37134)). His fix for a monotonous run of loosely joined sentences is to recast them with semicolons, periodic sentences or three-clause sentences, "whichever best represent the real relations of the thought."

Strunk also diagnoses the owner's complaint precisely, though about a word rather than a mark. In his entry on "while" he says writers use it as a substitute for "and" or "but", "either from a mere desire to vary the connective, or from uncertainty which of the two connectives is the more appropriate. In this use it is best replaced by a semicolon" ([Strunk 1920](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/37134)). **An all-purpose connective lets the writer avoid deciding what the relation is**, and the reader inherits that undecided relation. An em dash used between every point and its elaboration fails the same way.

Strunk & White (White's additions, in copyright) give each mark a job. The colon "tells the reader that what follows is closely related to the preceding clause", and two independent clauses take a colon "if the second interprets or amplifies the first." The dash is "a mark of separation stronger than a comma, less formal than a colon, and more relaxed than parentheses", and the rule ends with the sentence most directly on point: **"Use a dash only when a more common mark of punctuation seems inadequate."** Its worked examples take sentences strung together with dashes and rewrite them with commas and periods (Strunk & White, 4th ed., Rules 7 and 8 (Strunk and White, *The Elements of Style*, 4th ed., Allyn & Bacon, 2000)).

Williams treats punctuation as a set of choices with different effects: "punctuation is not governed by rules, but by choices." He lists ten ways to end one grammatical sentence and begin the next, each with a different effect, and assigns meanings to the less common ones. Readers take a colon "as shorthand for to illustrate, for example, that is, therefore"; a dash "suggests a casual afterthought"; parentheses carry "a sotto voce aside" (Williams & Bizup, Appendix I, pp. 209 to 223 (Williams and Bizup, *Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace*, 11th ed., Pearson, 2014)). That is a fuller statement of the owner's principle, with one difference to report honestly: Williams accepts the dash as a legitimate stylistic choice for an afterthought or for setting off an interruption that contains commas, and says paired commas, parentheses or dashes all work for long interruptions. Google narrows the em dash to a break or interruption in a sentence, and says that between an item and its description writers should "use a colon or a period" instead of a dash ([Google, Dashes](https://developers.google.com/style/dashes)). Google also says "If possible, avoid using semicolons", keeping them for closely related clauses where a period or comma is less effective and before words like "therefore" ([Google, Semicolons](https://developers.google.com/style/semicolons)). That puts Google at odds with Strunk's view of the semicolon, which matters for an owner who wants variety. Microsoft's tip on dashes covers only spacing ([Microsoft, Top 10 tips](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/style-guide/top-10-tips-style-voice)).

The evidence on AI prose supports the owner's diagnosis. Wikipedia's editors list em-dash overuse as an AI sign from 2022 to September 2026, observing that models used them "in places where humans are more likely to use commas, parentheses, colons", which describes one mark covering several relations ([Wikipedia: Signs of AI writing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing)). The same page then reports the cost of a bare ban: models hard-coded against em dashes stopped using "any sort of alternate punctuation", so commas and periods now appear more often than in human text, which "has become its own sign of AI writing." That claim is tagged citation needed. **The lesson for the skill is to state the rule by relation, not by prohibition alone.** Anthropic's own prompting guidance agrees: tell the model what to do rather than what not to do ([Anthropic, Prompting best practices](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices)). Applied to the owner's rule, that means a colon when the second part explains, illustrates or lists what the first announces; a semicolon when two closely related statements stand side by side; a comma with a conjunction ("because", "so", "but") when a word should name cause, consequence or contrast; parentheses for an aside the sentence could do without; and a period when each statement deserves its own weight. One inference follows: if the agent swaps every em dash for a colon, it has made the colon the new all-purpose mark and the problem returns, so the reference table must make the relation, not the replacement, the thing chosen.

## The sources split structure from style along a line Williams drew

The sources divide sentence style from document structure clearly, and that division favours two skills. Williams makes the division explicit: Part Two, "Clarity", covers "features of sentences and passages", and Part Three, "Clarity of Form", turns to "paragraphs, sections, and whole documents" (Williams & Bizup (Williams and Bizup, *Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace*, 11th ed., Pearson, 2014)). The FPLG separates "Organize" from "Write your document" in its table of contents, and ISO 24495-1's principles divide the same way, with "relevant" and "findable" governing readers, content selection and headings, and "understandable" governing the wording ([FPLG](https://centerforplainlanguage.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/FederalPLGuidelines.pdf); [plainlanguage.com](https://plainlanguage.com/what-is-plain-language/iso-plain-language-standard/)). The literatures themselves specialize: Strunk's 1918 book covers only "the paragraph and the sentence" and has no planning rule, Orwell stays at the level of words and images, and Minto, BLUF, the inverted pyramid, Diátaxis and DITA say nothing about sentences ([Strunk](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/37134); [Orwell](https://www.orwellfoundation.com/the-orwell-foundation/orwell/essays-and-other-works/politics-and-the-english-language/)). The two also happen at different moments: structure is decided before drafting, and style is applied while drafting and in Williams's diagnose, analyze and rewrite revision pass.

The case for one skill is real. Most single-volume guides (Williams, Strunk & White, the FPLG, Google, Microsoft) cover both, and Strunk & White files "Choose a suitable design and hold to it" in the same chapter as the sentence rules. Williams says one principle applies "to individual sentences, to substantive paragraphs, to sections and subsections, and to wholes": open with a short segment that frames what follows. Point-first has a sentence-scale twin in the FPLG's "Place the main idea before exceptions and conditions" and Microsoft's "Front-load keywords for scanning." A three-line commit message needs both kinds of rule at once.

**The recommendation is two skills**, for three reasons. The build-skills conventions require that "each skill's purpose fits one sentence with no 'and'" and keep SKILL.md to about seventy lines; "order a document so the reader gets what they need first" and "write sentences a reader understands on first reading" are two purposes, and the candidate rules below fill about seventy lines each. The motivating failures also split cleanly: a "findings" heading followed by a bare list is a structure failure, while clipped phrasing and an em dash doing every job are sentence failures. And the triggers differ, so an agent outlining a README and an agent revising one sentence load what each needs. The cost of splitting is handled by assigning the shared principle by scale: the structure skill owns point-first at document, section and paragraph scale, the style skill owns old-before-new at sentence scale, and each names the other under "Not here". The precedence rule goes into both as rule 1, since either skill can load alone in someone else's repo.

## The project's own guide wins, and artifacts and AI tells go in references

Precedence has a published model, and only one. Google's guide says: "Follow style guidance specific to your project or product. If project-specific style guidelines don't provide explicit guidance, then follow this guide. If the preceding references don't provide explicit guidance, then see these third-party references", and adds "This guide contains guidelines, not rules. Depart from it when doing so improves your content" ([Google developer documentation style guide](https://developers.google.com/style)). It is CC BY 4.0, the target repo's own licence, so it can be adopted nearly verbatim with attribution. The git project supplies the practical half for commit messages: run `git log --no-merges` on the files you are changing to see the current conventions ([git SubmittingPatches](https://git-scm.com/docs/SubmittingPatches)). Treating a repo's observed conventions (its log, its existing docs) as "project guidance" is an inference; Google's wording says only "style guidance specific to your project." Microsoft, ISO and the FPLG say nothing on precedence that the research could find. The owner's own preferences, such as no em dashes, belong in the skill rules for the owner's repos and give way to a project's documented style elsewhere, which the precedence rule already handles.

Conventions for commit messages, PR descriptions, issues and READMEs are applications of the structure rules, so they belong in a `references/` file for the structure skill rather than in its rules. Commit messages converge on a short summary line of about 50 characters, a blank line, an imperative mood ("If applied, this commit will..."), and a body that explains why ([Beams](https://cbea.ms/git-commit/); [Pope](https://tbaggery.com/2008/04/19/a-note-about-git-commit-messages.html)). Git adds that the problem is described in the present tense ("You do not have to say 'Currently'") and conflicts with Beams on capitalizing after an `area:` prefix, which the repo's log settles ([git SubmittingPatches](https://git-scm.com/docs/SubmittingPatches)). PR descriptions get a standalone first line written "as though it were an order" and a body for decisions not visible in the code ([Google eng-practices](https://google.github.io/eng-practices/review/developer/cl-descriptions.html)); GitHub asks for "why the change is needed, what changed, and where reviewers should pay special attention" ([GitHub Docs](https://docs.github.com/en/pull-requests/collaborating-with-pull-requests/getting-started/helping-others-review-your-changes)). Issues follow Mozilla: a summary that states "the problem, not your suggested solution", steps to reproduce, expected and actual results, and facts kept apart from speculation ([Mozilla](https://bugzilla.mozilla.org/page.cgi?id=bug-writing.html)). READMEs answer GitHub's five questions: what the project does, why it is useful, how to get started, where to get help, who maintains it ([GitHub Docs, About READMEs](https://docs.github.com/en/repositories/managing-your-repositorys-settings-and-features/customizing-your-repository/about-readmes)). Conventional Commits is a machine-readable prefix scheme for changelogs and version bumps; it says nothing about prose and should apply only where a repo already uses it ([Conventional Commits 1.0.0](https://www.conventionalcommits.org/en/v1.0.0/)).

The documented AI tells belong in a review checklist in the style skill's `references/`, not in its rules. Wikipedia's editors warn that their list is "descriptive, not prescriptive" and ask readers not to "merely treat these signs as the problems to be fixed; that could just make detection harder" ([Wikipedia: Signs of AI writing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing)). Anthropic says that a general instruction such as "avoid a generic AI look" "mostly swaps one default for another", and that models respond to instructions naming specific patterns ([Anthropic, Prompting Opus 5.5](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/prompting-claude-opus-5-5)). The structural patterns are stable enough to list: inline-header bullet lists, mechanical bold, title-case headings, the rule of three, negative parallelisms ("not X, but Y", "Y rather than X"), "-ing" tails, "serves as" in place of "is", correspondence phrases such as "I hope this helps", and closing summaries. The rules themselves stay positive and reader-centred, and the checklist is what a reviewer runs afterwards. OpenAI's Model Spec adds formatting "used judiciously" and "a direct answer rather than a list of facts" ([OpenAI Model Spec](https://model-spec.openai.com/2026-08-18.html)), and Google adds "Introduce a list with a complete sentence" ([Google, Lists](https://developers.google.com/style/lists)).

## Candidate rules, with sources and licence terms

The licence column uses three terms. **Quote** means the source is public domain, CC0, or CC BY 3.0 or 4.0 (or MIT), so short or adapted text can go into a CC-BY-4.0 repo with attribution and, for CC BY 3.0 and MIT, a note of that licence. **Paraphrase** means the text is in copyright with all rights reserved, share-alike (CC BY-SA, which would carry its terms into any adapted text), non-commercial and no-derivatives (CC BY-NC-ND), or under a licence of unclear compatibility; restate the idea in original words and cite it. A short, attributed, marked quotation from a paraphrase-only source is ordinary fair use in the US, but the skill should not depend on one. This is not legal advice. Rule wording below is a proposal in original words.

## What is deliberately left out, and why

**The blanket passive ban** is out. Pullum shows the stylistic case against the passive is "entirely baseless", Strunk & White misidentify passives in three of their four examples, and Orwell's own essay is heavier in passives than typical prose ([Pullum 2009](https://www.lel.ed.ac.uk/~gpullum/50years.pdf); [Pullum, passive paper](https://pullum.ppls.ed.ac.uk/passive_loathing.html)). AI text already underuses agentless passives, so the ban would push it further in the wrong direction. Rule 3 above keeps the sound part, which is about who acts, not grammatical voice. AR 25-50's "using the active voice" requirement is left out for the same reason.

**Usage folklore** is out: no "which" in restrictive clauses, no "however" or "and" or "but" to start a sentence, no split infinitives, singular "none". Pullum shows each is contradicted by the usage of good writers, and Williams's first principle is "Distinguish real grammatical rules from folklore" (Williams & Bizup (Williams and Bizup, *Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace*, 11th ed., Pearson, 2014)).

**Banned-word lists** are out of the rules. Wikipedia's AI vocabulary changes with each model generation ("delve" and "tapestry" in 2023, "foster" and "leverage" by mid-2026), so a fixed list goes stale, and its editors warn that treating signs as the problem only makes detection harder ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing)). Structural patterns go in the reference checklist instead.

**Fixed sentence-length limits and controlled vocabularies** are out. ASD-STE100's 20-word and 25-word caps and its roughly 900-word dictionary suit aircraft maintenance manuals but would make developer prose stilted ([ASD](https://www.asd-europe.org/standards-specifications/simplified-technical-english/what-are-the-basics-of-simplified-technical-english)); AR 25-50's 15-word average is in the same family. Wikipedia's mid-2026 entry reports that a "strict 8 to 20 word limit" has itself become an AI sign, though that entry rests on a single weak source. The FPLG warns against writing down to readers: "Don't write for an 8th grade class if your audience is composed of PhD candidates."

**Untestable maxims** are out: "Be clear", "Do not overwrite", "Do not explain too much". Pullum calls them vapid or tautologous, and no agent can check them.

**House mechanics** are left to the project's guide through the precedence rule: American spelling, the serial comma, bold UI labels, Standard Readme's fixed section order, Conventional Commits prefixes, and commit-subject capitalization. Sentence-case headings are the one mechanic kept as a default, because Google and Microsoft agree and title case is a documented AI tell. **Microsoft's em-dash spacing tip** is out because its only concern is spacing, not which relation the mark expresses. **Amazon's working backwards** supports rule 2 but is cited only through secondary sources, so it should not carry the rule.

## Gaps and unverified claims

Several primary texts were not read. Zinsser's *On Writing Well* is known here only through secondary summaries, so its wording and chapter structure are unverified and it should not be quoted. Minto's book was not read; her grouping rules and the "three to seven items" figure come from summaries, and the McKinsey origin story has no primary source. ISO 24495-1 is paid and its site blocked automated fetches, so its four principles and guideline lists come from summaries; the skill must say "after ISO 24495-1" and not claim conformance. The ASD-STE100 rule text, van der Meij and Carroll's 1995 minimalism paper, and the Bryar and Carr book on Amazon's practice were also not read.

Several licence facts are inferred rather than confirmed. That AR 25-50 and the FPLG are public domain rests on the general rule for US federal works; the official AR 25-50 link returned 404 and a Maine state copy was used, and the Center for Plain Language repost of the FPLG was not checked for its own notice. Git's SubmittingPatches is assumed to be GPLv2. Strunk's and Orwell's death years (and so their status in life-plus-70 countries) come from general knowledge. The live Microsoft guide's licence is unclear because its CC BY 4.0 repo is archived and the live source is private.

Several AI-prose claims are weak. Wikipedia's report that em-dash bans have reduced punctuation variety is tagged citation needed, the July 2026 study it cites (attributed to *The Economist*, finding Claude used em dashes more than professional writers) was not fetched, and the mid-2026 vocabulary entry rests on one review site. No source names the clipped, article-dropping phrasing the owner has seen as a failure mode, and no study measures how readable AI-written commit messages or PR descriptions are; the PNAS work measures how detectable the prose is, not how well readers understand it.

Covey has no writing-specific text from Covey himself in this research; a FranklinCovey business-writing style guide surfaced only as an unverified mirror. The report treats Habit 2 as a general principle that applies to writing directly, as asked, and pairs it with Minto and BLUF for the mechanics. Steven Pinker's *The Sense of Style* and Google's and Microsoft's full guides beyond the pages cited were not consulted. Finally, almost none of the candidate rules has reader-testing evidence behind it; the support is convergence among experts and standards bodies, plus Pullum's corpus counts and the PNAS measurements on AI text. The punctuation passages from Strunk, Strunk & White, Williams and Google, and the FranklinCovey Habit 2 page, were checked directly on 2026-10-26 to fill a gap in the notes.

## Conclusion

The research changes the shape of the problem more than it changes the rules. The rules an agent needs were mostly settled long ago and agree across traditions. What an agent most needs added is guidance on how to apply them: where one skill ends and the other begins, which rules give way to a project's own guide, and how to phrase a prohibition so it does not create a new tell. The em-dash case shows this most clearly. The owner's ban is sound because every major source chooses punctuation by the relation between clauses, but a ban stated alone produces the uniform, comma-and-period prose that Wikipedia's editors now flag. The rule is complete only when it names what to use for each relation.

The AI-prose evidence also changes which classic rules matter most. Nominalizations, "-ing" tails and copula avoidance are measured AI excesses, so Williams's actor and action rules work as fixes, while the passive ban is the one famous rule that would push AI text further from human prose. A skill built from Williams's checkable principles, with Covey, Minto and BLUF framing the structure and Google's precedence order deferring to the project, would be shorter than most style guides and better aimed at the failures agents actually produce.
